0 — correction

Two things I framed wrong

You made two arguments and both land. They change the verdicts on this page, so they go first.

your point 1

“We never share anything”

On-device processing, nothing uploaded.

This is a much stronger position than I gave it credit for, and it genuinely removes most of the risk column. No server copy means no breach, no third-party sharing, no leak, and none of the scandal patterns that have taken down apps in this space.

your point 2

“Not on day 1”

Permission comes after the fun, not before it.

Agreed — and this is what the page already argues in section 1. If nobody is asked for anything at first touch, the funnel objection disappears entirely for every tier above zero. We're saying the same thing here.

But here is the distinction that survives — and it's the important one

“We never share it” answers a privacy objection. It does not answer an access objection, and four of the rows below are access problems wearing privacy clothing:

  • Gmail needs a CASA audit to obtain the scope at all. What you later do with the data is irrelevant to whether Google will issue the token.
  • Spotify needs 250,000 MAU before it will serve you real data. Not a privacy rule — a business gate.
  • Slack needs a workspace admin to approve the install. Nothing to do with what you store.
  • Steps and workouts sit under App Store rule 5.1.3, which restricts use rather than transmission — so on-device doesn't change it. But read plainly the rule bars advertising and data mining, not game features, so this one is probably fine. It just needs a lawyer's eye, not a redesign.
  • Browser history — Chrome's new policy limits what an extension may collect to what's strictly necessary for its stated purpose. Collection, not sharing. And it doesn't exist on mobile at all.

Everything else moves in your favour. The revised table is below.

And "we never upload it" is worth more than a risk reduction

It's a marketing asset. The biggest instinctive objection to Boom is you're a company that watches my screen. A game that plays entirely on-device and asks for nothing on day one turns that objection inside out — and no competitor in the reward-game space can say it, because their whole model is harvesting.

1 — still true

For cold TikTok traffic the answer is: nothing

The question assumes personal data is what makes the game fun. Worth testing that assumption against the evidence first, because the evidence says otherwise.

The memory videos doing 4 to 30 million views — the ones I scraped, the whole reason this campaign exists — use no personal data at all. Coloured cards. Emoji. Objects on a table. Nothing personal, nothing connected, nothing granted.

Meanwhile every permission prompt costs you a large share of a cold funnel. Someone who arrived from a TikTok three seconds ago will not connect an account to play a game they haven't played yet.

So personal data is a retention layer, not an acquisition layer

Tier 0 — the thing 57.8 million people touch — must ask for zero. No login, no permission, no connect. A curated board, or the creator's board, played instantly in the browser.

Personal data is what you offer after they've played and enjoyed it. That's the moment a prompt is affordable, because you've already delivered the fun that pays for it.

The category everyone forgets

There's a third option between "no data" and "grant us access": data the user types instead of grants. Ask for a handle, not a permission. It feels completely personal, it costs nothing, and no review board is involved.

Three revisions to what I told you first — two in your favour

You pushed back on contacts and steps and you were largely right. I'd blanketed them as "no" without checking what the platforms actually built. Here is the corrected position on all three.

revision 01 · you were right, and my fix was wrong too

Steps and workouts: no privacy issue

You're right and I framed it badly. There is nothing private about a step count. "You walked 6,400 steps" is banal. A logged run is banal. I wrote "extreme risk" when what I actually meant was "there's an Apple contract term here" — those are different things and I collapsed them.

And the workaround I gave you doesn't work either. I said use CoreMotion instead of HealthKit. I've now read the actual guideline. App Store rule 5.1.3 names "Motion and Fitness" in the same sentence as HealthKit — both paths are covered by the identical restriction, so switching APIs escapes nothing.

What the rule actually says: you may not use that data "for advertising, marketing, or other use-based data mining purposes." Read plainly, a game board the user opted into is not advertising and not data mining — it's a feature they asked for. So steps and workouts are probably fine.

The one place it gets close to the line: tying step data to cash-prize eligibility. That starts to look like using health data to drive a commercial mechanic. Keep the steps as the puzzle and the prize as a flat game reward — don't make the number determine the payout — and get a lawyer to read 5.1.3 once before submission. That's the whole risk, and it's a submission question, not a privacy question.

revision 02 · partly right

Contacts: Apple built the safe path

My objection was that contacts expose people who never consented. That was right about the old all-or-nothing prompt — and iOS 18 fixed exactly that. There is now a contactAccessPicker and a Contact Access Button: the user picks specific contacts, the same pattern as the photo picker, and apps no longer need full address-book access at all.

So the API risk is largely handled, and "who did you add most recently" or "match the name to the number" is genuinely fun.

The residual risk isn't the API, it's the output. If a contact's name appears on the score card, every screenshot of your game leaks a third party who never used Boom. Rule: names can appear during play, never in anything shareable.

revision 03 · no, and worse than I thought

Browser history: don't touch it

It's the funniest data on the list — "what did you google at 2am" would print. That is precisely the problem: the funny material is the sensitive material. Health searches, job hunting, legal trouble, porn.

But the practical objections settle it before the ethics do. On mobile it does not exist — iOS blocks it outright, and on Android the only routes are accessibility-service tricks that read as spyware and get you removed. So it is desktop-extension-only, which is the same dead end as the desktop screen: useless for a mobile-first campaign.

And it puts your existing extension at risk. Chrome Web Store policy updated on 1 July 2026 with enforcement from 1 August — extensions may now only collect data strictly necessary for their stated purpose, closing exactly the loophole this would use. A screen recorder harvesting browsing history for a game is the textbook case. Risking the extension listing — a real distribution channel — for a game feature is a bad trade at any odds.

The Spotify finding is worth pausing on

Music was my instinct — Wrapped proved personal music data is the most viral format of this kind ever made, and music taste carries almost no privacy risk. Then I checked. Since February 2026, Spotify restricts Development Mode to non-commercial personal projects, and Extended Quota Mode requires a registered business with at least 250,000 monthly active users.

So you need the audience before you can build the thing that gets you the audience. It's closed to us, and I'd have led you into a dead end if I hadn't looked.

3 — the two free ones

What to actually build

free · zero permission

Type your handle

"This is your own feed. How well do you know it?"

They type @theirname. You pull their public posts and build a board from it — which of your posts got the most likes, which photo did you post first, what did you tweet on this date.

No permission, no OAuth, no review, no liability. And it feels more personal than a permission prompt, because they can see exactly what you used.

Best version: for the vibe-coder funnel, public GitHub — "guess your own commit count", "which repo got the most stars". That audience will post the result themselves.

free · zero permission

Pick four photos

"Four of your photos. Put them in order."

The system picker means no permission dialog and no library access — you only ever receive the four they tapped. Order them by date, guess what's in one, count the people in it.

Warmest, broadest format available. Works for people with no interest in tech at all, which is most of the audience wave 1 reaches.

Still needs the discipline: on-device only, share the score not the photo, and never enumerate the library even if the platform would let you.

worth the 10-day review

Your calendar, counted

"Guess how many hours you spent in meetings last month."

Same shock mechanic as screen time, and it is exactly our brand — "skip the meeting, send a Boom." The reveal is a number people find genuinely upsetting, which is what makes it travel.

Only read counts and durations. Never titles, never attendees, never content. That keeps it defensible and keeps other people's data out of it.

Where it belongs: the work funnel, not TikTok. This is the one that turns a game player into a recorder user.

4 — the rule

Ask for the smallest thing that still feels personal

The pattern across all of this: the perceived personalisation matters far more than the amount of data.

Four photos the user picked feels more personal than a full library scan, because they watched themselves choose them. A typed handle feels more personal than an OAuth grant, because they can see exactly what you're using. And neither costs you a review, an audit, or a liability.

Every source in the red rows of that table buys you more data and less trust. That is the wrong trade for a company whose core product is watching people's screens — we are the last company that can afford to look greedy about data.

The order I'd build in
  • Wave 1: zero data. Curated and creator boards only.
  • Then: typed handle → public data. Free, fast, no review.
  • Then: photo picker. Free, but ship the privacy rules with it.
  • Then: steps and workouts. One light prompt, nothing private in the answer — just keep the prize flat rather than driven by the number.
  • Then, for the work funnel only: calendar counts. Start the Google review now if you want it in a month — it's ~10 days plus your own build time.
  • Optional: contacts via the iOS 18 picker — only if names never reach a shareable card.
  • Never: Gmail, Slack, browser history.
And keep the "we ask for nothing" line

Every competitor in the reward-game space demands accounts and permissions. If Boom's game plays with zero grants, that is a real differentiator worth saying out loud — and it makes the recorder's privacy story credible at the same time.
