The Work Mask

A game at your desk is forbidden. A screen recorder is not. Boom is the only cash game you can have open at work without looking like you're slacking — because the app genuinely is a work tool. That's a reason to install on desktop that has nothing to do with wanting a recorder.

1 — why this matters

It fixes the exact step the model says is broken

The funnel model has one catastrophic step: a TikTok memory-game player installing a desktop screen recorder. I put it at 2% and said it might be generous, because a person who just played a game on their phone is not shopping for work software.

That was the right analysis of the wrong pitch. "Install our screen recorder" gives them no reason. "The game is better on your desktop, and it's the one you're allowed to have open at work" gives them a real one — and it has nothing to do with wanting a recorder.

The recorder stops being the thing you're selling and becomes the thing that makes the game permissible.

Why the camouflage is real, not a joke

Solitaire and Minesweeper shipped with Windows and defined desk culture for twenty years. Wordle grew on office workers. The work-break game is one of the most durable consumer categories there is — and it has one permanent problem: you are not supposed to be playing it.

A game in your dock is a confession. A screen recorder in your dock is unremarkable. Over a shoulder, on a shared screen, in a standup — Boom reads as work. No other cash game can say that, and none of them can build it, because they aren't also a work tool.

And it flips which product drives daily use

A screen recorder is opened maybe four times a month. A work-break game is opened daily — call it twenty sessions. The free tier earns from ads, and ad revenue scales with sessions, not signups.

So the same user is worth roughly five times more on the free tier, without paying you a cent more. The game isn't a marketing stunt for the recorder — it's the thing that makes the recorder a daily habit instead of an occasional utility.

3 — the pitch

How to actually say it

to the phone player

After their first score

"Better on your desktop — and it's the one game that looks like work."

Not "download our screen recorder." The desktop version has bigger boards, the leaderboard, the prize draw — and it lives in a window nobody questions. Say the quiet part; that is the pitch.

to the office

The social proof line

"Your whole team can play. Nobody has to explain it."

Work-break games spread horizontally through offices — that's how Wordle and Minesweeper moved. One person installs, the leaderboard makes it visible, and the fact that it's a legitimate tool removes the friction that normally stops it.

to the buyer

Never say any of the above

"It will not lose your recording."

The team lead evaluating Boom must never meet the work-mask pitch. Same app, opposite message. This is the two-audience split from the strategy memo, now living on one desktop surface — and it needs a real wall between the two.

4 — the risks

Two ways this backfires

1. Corporate IT blocks it

Cash prizes on a managed work machine is a recognised policy category. Some IT departments will block the installer outright, and a few will treat it as gambling-adjacent software. The very corporate legitimacy you're borrowing is what puts it in front of an IT policy in the first place.

Ironically, the bigger the campaign works, the more likely someone in IT notices.

2. It poisons the B2B sale

A team lead who installs Boom as a work tool, then discovers it's a cash-prize game, may distrust the whole product. "Is this serious software?" is a hard objection to recover from — and it's the objection your paying customers would raise.

The fix that solves both: an admin switch

Make the game a surface inside the app that a workspace admin can turn off, and ship Boom with it off by default on team plans, on by default on personal accounts.

That single toggle does a lot of work:

  • IT can approve the recorder without approving a game — so the installer stops being a policy problem
  • The B2B buyer never sees it, so the "is this serious" objection never fires
  • The consumer keeps the full loop, prizes included
  • And "your admin can disable it" is a sentence that makes the whole thing sound considered rather than sneaky

Build the toggle before wave 1, not after the first angry IT email.

5 — honesty

What's evidence and what's argument

Evidence: that the desktop install step is the weakest link — that comes out of the funnel model. That work-break games are a large and durable category — Solitaire, Minesweeper, Wordle. That ad revenue on a free tier scales with sessions rather than signups.

Argument: that the work-mask framing lifts installs from 2% to 6%. That is a guess with a plausible mechanism, not a measurement — I picked 6% because it's a threefold improvement from giving someone an actual reason, and the table shows what happens at other values so you can substitute your own. Wave 1 replaces it with a real number.
