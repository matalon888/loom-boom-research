1 — the raw materials

What one picked photo actually hands you

Three separate sources, all available on-device, none requiring a permission dialog beyond the picker itself.

source 01 · exif

Metadata

Date and exact time taken. Day of week. Device model. Orientation. Flash fired or not. Whether it's a Live Photo or part of a burst. Camera settings — focal length, ISO, exposure.

Free, instant, and precise. This is where the best questions come from, because time is the thing people are most wrong about.

source 02 · on-device vision

What's in the picture

Apple's Vision framework and Android's ML Kit both run locally: face detection and count, scene and object classification, text recognition, pet detection, dominant colours, and saliency — which region of the image the eye goes to.

Nothing is uploaded. The model ships with the OS. Face count is highly reliable; object labels are decent; scene classification is rough.

source 03 · the set

Relationships between photos

If they pick four, you get the gaps between them, the order, the spread, which was the outlier, whether two were taken seconds apart or years apart.

This is the cheapest source and it powers the most reliable game — ordering. No vision model, no risk, works on any photo that has a date.

3 — the one to build first

"How long ago was this?" is the whole game

If you only ship one question, ship number 1. It has a property none of the others do: the owner of the photo cannot win.

You took the picture. You were there. And you will still guess "about a year ago" about something from 2021. That gap between certainty and reality is the entire emotional payload, and it fires every single time, on every photo, forever. There is no content to run out of.

It's also the safest. The answer is a number of months. No faces, no objects, no location, no model that might be wrong — just a date subtraction that is always correct.

The scoring that makes it shareable

Don't score right or wrong. Score how wrong — "you were off by 3 years and 2 months." Sum the error across five photos and that total is the score.

Lower is better, it's comparable between any two people regardless of which photos they used, and it produces a single number that means something instantly: "I was off by 11 years total."

Round structure I'd use

Five photos. Each: show it, ask "how long ago", they drag a slider, reveal the true date with the error. Then a final card with the total. Whole thing under 45 seconds.

4 — the share card

The shareable artifact is your wrongness, not your photo

This is the design decision that makes the whole thing safe, and it happens to make it more viral rather than less.

A score card that says "off by 11 years, 4 months" contains no image, no face, no location, no name. Nothing to redact, nothing to leak, nothing anyone can regret posting. And it is more compelling than the photo would have been, because it's a number people want to beat.

Same logic for every question in the bank: the output is the error, the streak, the score — never the input.

Rules for whoever builds it
  • Never render a picked photo into a share image. Not blurred, not cropped, not as a thumbnail. If it's shareable, it has no photo in it.
  • Never upload the photo. EXIF read and vision run on-device; only the score is a network call.
  • Drop location by default. Question 20 is the one field that can expose a home address. Ship without it, add it later behind an explicit opt-in if it tests well.
  • Discard on exit. No cache, no temp directory that survives the session.
5 — the bridge

Why a photo game sells a screen recorder

Worth being explicit about this, because it's the obvious hole: a photo memory game does not obviously have anything to do with recording screens.

The link is the engine. Flash → recall → score → share is one mechanic, and it doesn't care what the image is. Photos are the mass-market on-ramp because everyone has them and nobody needs an explanation. Screens are the same game with different material — and that version is the one that sells the recorder.

The progression
  • Your photos — anyone can play, no explanation needed. This is the TikTok version.
  • Your screenshots — same engine, funnier, more "how did you know that about me".
  • A product demo — now it's a watch-through test, and now it's a work tool.

One engine, three materials, increasing commercial value. Build it once for photos and the other two are content, not code.

6 — caveats

Things to check before building

  • Location metadata may not arrive. The system pickers are designed to hand over as little as possible, and location handling has changed repeatedly across OS versions. Verify what you actually receive before designing question 20 — and my recommendation is to skip it regardless.
  • Not every photo has a date. Screenshots, saved WhatsApp images, downloads and anything re-encoded may have no EXIF at all. Since questions 1–8 all depend on it, you need a graceful fallback — detect missing EXIF at pick time and route those photos to the vision questions instead.
  • Vision reliability is uneven. Face count is dependable. Pet detection is good. Generic object counting is not — whitelist the classes that actually work rather than trusting the label.
  • Four photos is a lot to ask. Each pick is friction. Consider asking for one and generating several questions from it, rather than asking for four and generating one.
  • Nobody has tested whether this is fun twice. The whole bank is design, not evidence. Question 1 is the one I'd bet on because its emotional payload doesn't decay — but that's an argument, not a result.
