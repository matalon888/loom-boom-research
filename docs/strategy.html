02 — the actual asymmetry

Everyone else charges the user. You pay the user.

This is the one thing about Boom that no competitor in the field can copy — not because it's clever, but because their business models forbid it.

Loom's original growth engine was never marketing. It was a loop: you receive a Loom, you watch it, you want to reply the same way, you sign up. The recipient became the next creator, free, and the product spread through ordinary work.

In February 2026 Atlassian broke that loop. Free Creator Lite seats were deleted and auto-converted to paid. The recipient now hits a bill instead of a signup button. That is the single largest strategic event in this category and nobody has moved into the gap.

Boom already has the mechanism to rebuild the loop — and to make it stronger than Loom's ever was, because Boom's version puts money on the recipient side of the arrow instead of a paywall.

Why nobody can follow you here

Tella, Screen Studio, Cap are bootstrapped and tiny — $5M ARR, $30k MRR, three-person teams. They cannot fund payouts.

Veed and Descript could afford it and never will — paying creators would cannibalise $50M+ subscription businesses built on the user paying them.

Loom is inside Atlassian, which just spent a year moving in the exact opposite direction.

Structural immunity is rare. When you find one, you build the whole strategy on it rather than treating it as a feature.

03 — the reframe

Stop buying clicks. Buy views from the people who make them.

The same $13.35, two ways

Spent on Google, $13.35 buys one visitor to your site, once, and you find out afterwards whether they mattered.

Spent as creator credits, $13.35 buys a creator who then distributes your links to their own audience — and you only pay once the views already happened. The spend is downstream of the result instead of a bet placed before it.

This is the part worth being precise about, because it's also where the idea can go wrong. Paying for raw views buys you view-farming, not customers. The unit you pay on has to be the unit you actually want.

  • Don't pay per view. Pay per distinct authenticated viewer, or per viewer who returns, or per referred signup that activates.
  • Cap per creator per period so the ceiling is knowable and the fraud incentive stays small.
  • Weight by watch-through — which also happens to be the metric nobody else in the category can even report on.

Get that right and your acquisition line becomes performance-based by construction. Get it wrong and you've built a bot farm with a nice UI.

04 — the message problem

Two audiences. One product. Do not mix the copy.

This resolves the tension I flagged in the first memo — that nobody in 1,795 complaints asked to be paid. Both things are true, because there are two different people involved.

Audience A — the buyer

The Loom refugee

“It will not lose your recording.”

Founders, agencies, course creators, support and product teams. They arrived because Loom capped them, billed them for viewers, or ate a 15-minute presentation. They want reliability, no caps, their own MP4, and no login wall for the person watching. Money never appears in this message. This is who retains and, eventually, who pays for a team tier.

Audience B — the distributor

The earner

“You already record. Now it pays.”

Creators, students, side-hustle audiences, makers building in public. They don't care about async work culture — they care that a thing they already do now returns something. They are not the customer; they are the channel. Their posts, their links, and their payout screenshots are what bring Audience A in.

What's wrong with the homepage today

“Pay Loom to record — or record and get paid with Boom” is Audience B's line, served to Audience A's traffic. Someone who just lost a client recording reads it and concludes Boom is a rewards app, not a tool. Split the surfaces: boomshare.ai speaks to A, and a separate earn surface — plus all of social — speaks to B.

You asked for a lot of video. The advantage here is that Boom's output is natively the format — so marginal cost per asset is close to zero, which is exactly what a high-volume posting strategy needs.

You have 1,795 of them, already collected, many with hundreds of upvotes and genuine stakes — lost job applications, lost lessons, lost client work. One record, twelve languages

Boom already dubs. Every clip becomes twelve clips for twelve language markets at no extra shoot. Competitors would have to pay for translation and re-voicing; you press a button. This is the only place where a product feature converts directly into a posting-volume advantage.

Payout receipts

“Here's what I earned” is the most reliably viral format on the internet, and it is the one piece of content your model produces automatically. Every payout is a post. Boom Match generates the identical asset, so the two products feed one content stream.

Nobody in the category is making this argument; it's unclaimed territory

Highest-fit creator segment in the market; the loop pays them, so no upfront sponsorship
06 — Boom Match

What actually transfers, and what doesn't

Worth being blunt here, because forcing two products together is a common way to weaken both.

Transfers — and it's the expensive part

  • Payout rails. Paying individuals real money at scale — KYC, tax, disbursement, dispute handling — is the hardest and slowest part of “we pay the user.” Boom Match has already paid for it.
  • Anti-fraud. Skill-cash gaming forces you to build serious abuse detection. Pay-per-view has exactly the same failure mode. This is the least obvious and most valuable overlap.
  • The Meta playbook. “Get paid for something you already do” is one creative thesis serving both products — same account, same audience-testing learnings, same proof formats.
  • Trust positioning. The post-Papaya credibility work applies verbatim the moment Boom Share promises payouts.
The one genuinely new idea: a shared wallet

One Boom balance. Earn it recording, spend or play it in Boom Match, cash out from either. Neither product has a retention loop that strong on its own, and it's a reason to open Boom on a day you have nothing to record.

Does not transfer — don't pretend otherwise

  • The audience. Boom Match is US skill-cash gamers. Boom Share is makers and teams. The overlap isn't a demographic, it's a moment — a person at a desk, at a computer, during a workday. That's real but thin. Don't build one funnel and expect both to convert.
  • The brand promise. “Your work is the puzzle” and “skip the meeting” are different products solving different needs. Shared wallet, shared rails, shared ad account — separate stories.
  • The channel mix. Boom Match's own research said Meta must be primary because search intent doesn't match product intent. Boom Share has the opposite problem: enormous search volume with the wrong intent. Same conclusion, different reason — but the campaigns aren't interchangeable.
07 — the hole

The question this plan does not yet answer

If everything is free forever and you also pay users, what pays for it?

This is the load-bearing unknown, and I don't have your numbers so I can't resolve it. Every other item in this memo is downstream of the answer, because the payout rate you can sustain determines whether the loop spins or bleeds.

The plausible answers, roughly in order of how well they fit what exists today:

  • A team tier. Audience A pays for shared workspaces, admin, retention, SSO. Free stays free for individuals; the payout is a customer-acquisition cost funded by team revenue. This is the most conventional and most likely correct answer.
  • Boom Match rake. Gaming revenue subsidises recording payouts, and recording brings the players. The shared wallet makes this mechanically clean.
  • Attention on the watch page. You'd be monetising the viewer, which is the same place Loom now extracts a seat fee — with the difference that yours pays the creator instead of charging the viewer. Risky for the work-tool audience; fine for the creator audience.

My recommendation is the first, with the second as the bridge. But this is the one decision I'd want settled before scaling payouts, rather than after.

08 — what I'd do

The sequence

Opinionated. The ordering matters more than any individual item — each step de-risks the next.

First

Settle the money question

Decide what funds payouts, and derive a payout rate you can defend at 100× current volume. Everything below assumes a number here.

Then

Port Boom Match's anti-fraud onto Boom Share views

Before a single payout ad runs. Pay on distinct authenticated viewers with per-creator caps, not raw views. This is the difference between a growth loop and a bot farm, and you already own the hard part.

Then

Split the two messages

Reliability-first hero on boomshare.ai for Audience A. A separate earn surface, and all of social, for Audience B. Stop serving B's copy to A's traffic.

Weeks 1–4

Run the “Loom lost it” series daily, in four languages

One clip a day from the corpus, dubbed. Cheapest, most differentiated content you will ever make, and it tests the reliability message and the video channel at the same time. Nobody in your lane is posting; 3,510 subscribers is the bar.

In parallel

Keep the cheap SEO floor

The Loom downloader and 25 comparison pages only. They compound quietly, they cost a week, and they catch the people already searching. Don't expand past that until the loop is proven.

Month 2

Recruit 50 vibe-coder creators onto the paid loop

People whose output is already screen recordings. Give them the tool, pay them per verified viewer, let their audience be the distribution. If the loop works at 50 creators, it works at 5,000 — and if it doesn't, you've learned it for the price of 50.

The one-line version

Everyone in this category is buying attention. Boom is the only one that can afford to pay for distribution instead — because it's the only one whose model already sends money in that direction. Social is how you announce it; the loop is the actual business.

09 — honesty note

What here is measured and what is judgement

Measured: the YouTube subscriber counts, the sitemap page counts, the CPC and volume figures, the 1,795-comment corpus and every quote drawn from it, and the February 2026 Atlassian seat change.

Judgement, not fact: that competitors are structurally unable to match payouts, that the paid loop will out-perform paid search, that the shared wallet improves retention, and the entire sequencing above. These are arguments from the evidence, not findings — worth arguing with, and worth testing at 50 creators before betting the company on.
