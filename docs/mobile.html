The Screen
Is a Screenshot

Live screen capture doesn't port to phones — iOS and Android both fight it. But the mobile analogue is sitting right there: everyone's phone already has a Screenshots album, and reading it needs no permission at all.

1 — the problem, stated properly

The campaign is 100% mobile. The game is desktop-only.

TikTok traffic is phones. Every one of the 57.8 million views in wave 1 arrives on a phone, taps a link on a phone, and decides in about three seconds. If what they land on is “download our desktop app”, the entire campaign leaks out before anyone plays anything.

So this has to be solved before wave 1 ships, not after. It's a wave 0 item.

Why the desktop mechanic can't just be ported

Both platforms deliberately make ambient screen access impossible. On iOS, ReplayKit needs a user-initiated recording with a system prompt every session. On Android, MediaProjection throws a consent dialog every time. Neither can run in the background, and neither is something a person will do to play a 15-second game.

It isn't a build problem you can engineer around. It's the platform's position, and it isn't changing.

2 — your option 1

Pulling from desktop is a good feature and the wrong answer

Using their desktop-captured screens inside the phone app works technically, and it's worth building — eventually.

But it can't be the mobile entry point, for one reason: a person arriving from TikTok has never opened the desktop app. There is no data to pull. Option 1 requires the user to already be a user, so it solves retention, not acquisition — and acquisition is the entire point of the campaign.

Ship it as tier 3, after the loop works.

Where it does earn its place

Once someone records on desktop, their real work screens become the best possible puzzle material — a Figma file, a codebase, a Slack thread. That's genuinely personal and impossible for any competitor to copy.

It's also the natural bridge between the two funnels: game player installs desktop → becomes recorder user. Just don't put it first.

3 — the answer

On a phone, the screen you play with is a screenshot

This is the whole design in one line. Screenshots are screens. Every phone has hundreds of them in a dedicated album. And getting at them costs nothing.

The permission situation is better than you'd expect

Apple's PHPickerViewController requires no photo-library permission and shows no allow-access dialog at all. It runs out of process; the app only ever receives the images the user taps. Apple calls it the gold standard for privacy and now pushes every developer to use it. Android's system photo picker behaves the same way.

So the most personal, most emotionally loaded material on the device is also the least gated. No prompt, no scary dialog, no drop-off.

What is genuinely blocked — don't plan around it

App-usage data is out on iOS. The Screen Time API deliberately never exposes usage figures to the developer — the data can only be rendered inside a system extension, and reading it into your app returns nothing.

On Android, UsageStatsManager needs PACKAGE_USAGE_STATS, a system-level permission that isn't granted to third-party apps; the user must go into Settings and turn it on manually. That is far too much friction for a game.

But here's the trick that gets you the same content anyway. You can't read someone's Screen Time. You can ask them to screenshot it — which takes one gesture everybody already knows — and then play the game on that image.

That generalises. Anything on a phone that a person can see, they can screenshot in a second. So the input to Screen Recall on mobile is never an API. It's a picture the user took, handed over through a picker that needs no permission. Same on iOS and Android, no platform fights, no consent dialogs, and it is still literally a screen.

And it keeps the brand honest

Boom is a screen-recording company. On mobile, the thing people record is a screenshot. The mechanic doesn't get watered down to reach phones — it gets translated into the phone's own native version of itself.

4 — the ladder

Three tiers, each unlocking more personal material

The rule: friction rises only after fun is proven. A cold viewer plays before they install; installing buys personalisation; linking desktop buys their real work.

5 — the material

What on a phone is actually fun to be tested on

Ranked by how strong the emotional reaction is, because that is what gets a video reposted. All of these are screenshots the user takes and picks — no API, no permission.

01 · the shock

Guess your own screen time

“How many hours did you spend on your phone this week? Now look.”

People are genuinely, reliably horrified by their own number, and horror is the most shareable emotion there is. They screenshot the Screen Time page, guess first, then reveal. The gap between guess and truth is the content.

Why it wins: universal, self-incriminating, and the reveal is a number — which makes it trivially comparable between friends.

02 · the embarrassment

Rebuild your home screen

“Your home screen, 5 seconds. Now put the apps back.”

Nobody can do it. People look at that screen a hundred times a day and still cannot place their own icons. The failure is the joke, and everyone fails the same way.

Why it wins: zero setup, works on any phone on earth, and being bad at it is more fun than being good.

03 · the nostalgia

Your screenshots from last month

“What were you screenshotting in July?”

The Screenshots album is a diary nobody knows they're keeping — memes, flight prices, arguments, recipes, things you meant to come back to. Flash one, ask what was in it.

Why it wins: this is the closest thing to the desktop mechanic, and it is unlimited, free, and different for every single person.

04 · the count

How many unread?

“Guess your unread email badge. Off by 12,000?”

One screenshot of a home screen with badges. Guess each one. The people with 40,000 unread emails will post it themselves, because that's a personality now.

Why it wins: instantly legible in a thumbnail, which matters more on TikTok than the game itself.

05 · the order

Which came first?

“Four of your photos. Put them in order.”

Photos from the camera roll, ordered by date. Personal, warm, and it works for people who have no interest in tech at all — which is most of the audience wave 1 reaches.

Why it wins: broadest possible appeal, and it makes the app feel like it knows them.

06 · the crossover

Beat the creator's screen

“This is @creator's actual phone. Can you recall it?”

The creator's own screenshot becomes the board everyone plays. Their audience competes against them, on a leaderboard, with a prize.

Why it wins: this is what makes a paid post keep working after it's paid for — the board outlives the ad.

6 — the part that could destroy us

Screenshots are the most dangerous data on a phone

Say the risk plainly

People's Screenshots album contains DMs, bank balances, medical results, addresses, other people's faces and messages, and things they have forgotten are in there. If Boom uploads any of that to a server to render a public leaderboard, one bad incident ends the company. Not damages it — ends it.

This is the single highest-risk thing in the entire plan, and it is worth more engineering care than the game itself.

The rules I'd write into the spec before anyone builds it:

  • On-device only by default. The image never leaves the phone. The game runs locally; only the score is a network call.
  • User-picked, always. Never enumerate the library. The system picker enforces this for you — lean on it rather than asking for full access.
  • Share the score, not the screen. The default shareable artifact is a number and a blurred thumbnail. Sharing the actual image is a separate, explicit, per-image action.
  • Redact before any share. Run on-device text detection and blur anything that looks like an email, phone number, card, address or balance — and show the user what you blurred so they can check.
  • No cloud copy for prizes. If a leaderboard entry needs verification, verify the score, not the image.

Done this way, “we never see your screenshots” becomes a marketing line rather than a liability — and it is the exact opposite of what people fear about a company that records screens.

7 — what to build for wave 1

The minimum that makes the campaign work

Only tier 0 is genuinely required before the creators post. Everything else can land after — but tier 0 has to be perfect, because it is the three seconds the whole $4,550 is buying.

must

A web game that plays in the TikTok in-app browser

No install, no account, no permission. A curated screen — a messy desktop, a famous app UI, a fake home screen — flashed for five seconds, then three questions. Score at the end. This is the only thing wave 1 strictly needs.

must

Score screen that asks for one thing

After the score, a single offer: “play with your own screen” → app install, or “make one for your friends” → the share loop. One choice, not a menu.

must

Instrument it end to end

Play started, play finished, share tapped, install tapped, credit purchased. Wave 1 exists to produce the credit-conversion number — if this isn't wired, the $4,550 buys nothing but views.

next

Tier 1 in the app: photo picker + on-device play

Screenshots album, formats 01–05 above, all local. Ship with the privacy rules from section 6 built in from the first commit, not bolted on later.

later

Tier 2: pull from desktop — your option 1

Once someone records on desktop, their real work screens become boards on their phone. Best material in the product, and the bridge from game player to recorder user. It just can't come first.

8 — honesty

What's verified and what's design

Verified today: that PHPickerViewController needs no permission and shows no dialog, and that Apple now recommends it for all photo-picking. That the iOS Screen Time API never exposes usage data to the developer. That Android's UsageStatsManager requires a system-level permission users must grant in Settings. That live capture on both platforms requires per-session consent.

Design, not fact: the three-tier ladder, all six formats, and the claim that a screenshot is a satisfying substitute for a live screen. Nobody has tested whether guessing your own screen time is actually fun for more than one round — that's what tier 0 finds out. The privacy rules in section 6 are my recommendations, not a legal review; get one before shipping anything that touches a camera roll.
